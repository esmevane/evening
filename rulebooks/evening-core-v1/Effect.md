Players have a lot of discretion in how they direct their characters. Sometimes this discretion results in some remarkable moments of impressive ingenuity. Other times, not so much. In order to model out how appropriate (or inappropriate) an character's actions are, Evening uses *effect*.

**Effect level.** Effect has five levels which describe how much of the intended impact the action has. This ranges from *none*, which is just "no effect", to *devastating*, which is the most incisive an effect can get.

- *None.* It doesn't really matter if you succeeded or not, it just doesn't work. For example, you might be the best acrobat in the world but you still can't backflip your way through a steel vault door.
- *Minimal.* You tried, it worked. Kind of? You didn't necessarily pick the worst possible way to do it, but it's still kind of surprising that you pulled it off at all.
- *Standard.* You did it. You did it real normal.
- *Great.* You cleverly leveraged your abilities here, and not only did it work, it *really* worked. You have better than normal results.
- *Devastating.* You picked the perfect approach and you executed things flawlessly. It's possible that there would be no better way to do this. It's possible no one will ever do it this well again.

**Determining effect.** Whenever a character makes a check or uses a move, they describe what they're doing and what they hope to accomplish. The narrator then decides the scale of the difficulty, and the base effect level.

**Adjusting effect.** Some character features and game systems allow for characters to selectively adjust effect, after it's been determined. Generally, the player must adjust effect before making a check, unless the feature specifies otherwise.

**Taking effect.** Whenever a check succeeds, it takes effect. In most cases, this just lets the narrator know what to describe in terms of outcomes. However, some features that can take effect will describe what happens if they take a certain effect level.
