Evening is a game, and it's built around a random mechanic: a die with 20 sides, usually called a d20.

**Impartial tension.** An Evening game relies a lot on the idea of impartial tension. What this means is that a core part of every game session is the idea that when people disagree, or something seems hard, they can rely on the system itself to resolve things. The core component of managing this kind of impartial tension is the d20.

**Conflict and resolution.** It's okay to have disagreements and Evening wants you to leverage them dramatically, and that's where the d20 comes in. Whenever something seems implausible, or there's a challenge at hand, or there's risk to a player's goals, a d20 check can help.

- **Rolling.** Rolling higher is better, and lower rolls are worse. You want to get that 20, and you don't want to get that 1.
- **Target numbers.**  Whenever you roll a d20, you're aiming to get over a target number. If you roll equal to or over that number, you succeed. Otherwise, you fail.
- **Modifiers.** Characters and circumstances can influence your roll, adding or subtracting from the roll outcome.
	- **Bounded modifiers.** The sum of the bonuses or penalties to a d20 check cannot exceed +20 or -20. In other words, it doesn't matter how many bonuses you have to a check past a certain point.
- **Advantage and disadvantage.** Sometimes you'll have advantage or disadvantage on a check. In both cases, you roll two d20 instead of one.
	- **Advantage.** If you have advantage, you take the highest.
	- **Disadvantage.** If you have disadvantage, you take the lowest.
	- **Cancellation.** You consider all advantage and disadvantage factors before making a check. They cancel out. *For example*, if you have 3 things that give you advantage, and 2 things that give you disadvantage, you have advantage.
- **Critical rolls.** Whenever a check is 1 or 20, it's a critical. A 20 is a critical success, and always succeeds, and a roll of 1 is critical failure, which always fails.
- **Taking effect.** Most checks have an *effect*. That means that after they're made, if they succeed, there's a clear definition of how effective the check is. A successful check takes effect, and a failed check does not.

**Narrators (usually) don't roll.** Generally, Evening is built so that narrators don't make the checks, the players do. Narrators are in charge of deciding target numbers, and effect, and scale. That's a lot of work on its own!

There are scenarios where the narrator does need to get involved in making checks, but those are usually designed against, or they're voluntary (*for example*: the narrator is playing a character).

**Contests.** Sometimes two checks are made against one another. In these cases, the rules are the same, and the higher check wins. In the event of a tie, every participant in the contest re-rolls.

**Group checks.** In cases where a group must make a check together, at least half of the group (rounded up) must roll higher than the target number on their check. *For example*, in a group of three, in order to sneak past a guard, at least two members of the group must succeed in their stealth checks.

> **Design notes.** The d20 works for game design purposes because it sits in a math space that is clearly linked to percentiles, without *being* a percentile. Every side of a d20 represents a 5% chance, and every plus or minus modifies it by 5%.
> 
> This has a cascading outcome with regards to making up other systems that link up to the d20. It's easy to objectively ask yourself, "Does this seem fair?" in a world where you can see that a system adds or subtracts, for example, 10. That's +50% or -50%! It's a huge swing in the outcomes.
> 
> Several other approaches were considered, if only because it'd make Evening easier to play on the road. If we'd made things against a d6 pool, that'd make it easy to go to a convenience store and buy some dice. If we made it against a deck of cards, even simpler! Unfortunately, these all had dramatic downstream mechanical impacts for secondary systems that made stuff pretty hard to make a prescribed design against.
> 
> And so, for better or worse, we're going with the d20.
> 
> **Designing for the boundary.** The modifier boundary for any given check is +20 or -20. That means that you can't add or subtract more than 100% in modifiers alone. Games which scale indefinitely seem interesting in a hypothetical space, but in practice, they create a linear treadmill that paradoxically robs modifiers of their fun and perceived value.
> 
> Creating a boundary and establishing the "best" mark, on the other hand, does the opposite: it defines a limit and lets people know just how rad or inept they are. Weirdly, it winds up being more risky, and more fun!
