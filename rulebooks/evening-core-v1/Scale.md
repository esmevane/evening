Quick improvisation is a key part of narrating Evening games. In order to make this easier for a narrator to do, most mechanical layers are designed with a scale. Scale is a set of ranks, from 1-10, that determine how formidable something is.

**Scale describes a lot.** Scale maps to difficulty, but it's not necessarily just "how difficult something is". It can also tell you a lot, quickly, about a lot of different things.  For example:

- How much health a creature has, or how much its strongest and weakest attacks do. These can all be ranked differently!
- How powerful a character's moves become as they gain in strength.
- How dangerous a volcano is, or how tough the traps are in the volcano dungeon that the characters are trying to escape, or how much time the characters have left to escape.
- How powerful a faction is as an ally, and how dangerous they are as a foe.
- Whether or not something results in rewards, such as experience or resources.

**Per-layer significance.** Generally, any mechanical layer will line up with scale somehow. This lets you quickly gauge how to use the layer and incorporate it into the larger narrative, if you like.

**Scale is not balance.** It isn't useful as a way to govern relative balance. This sort of thing rarely holds in gameplay, anyway. One individual actor within the game - be it a danger, a character, or whatever - might have its own scale, and also have a menu of things it can do all with different scale ranks.

**Using scale.** Any time you need to make something up quickly, as a narrator, decide on a scale rank. Take a look at the scale table to see what that rank means, and either use the value you find there or adjust it a bit.

*For example:* A character tries to jump across a short gap. This seems like it's manageable, but risky. The narrator figures it's got a scale of 2, checks the scale table, and sees that the character must succeed on a check of 14 or higher to clear the gap. That actually seems a little high, so the narrator adjusts it down to 13 and tells the player to make the jump check against that.

**Detecting scale.** Players will know the scale of their own features and moves, and will see it in tension-building systems such as clocks. However, they won't necessarily know the scale of anything else unless they have some kind of feature that allows it. Figuring out how tough or easy something is can be a check in and of itself, or something granted by a feature.

**Interacting with scale.** Some scales can be adjusted with character interaction, such as through character features or with effect. In these cases, the move or feature will outline what causes the change in scale and how it works.

**Scale tables.** Consult the scale table whenever you need to map a scale rank to an outcome. Scale tables can vary based on layers, so sometimes layers will define their own table, and other times games might collect their own scale table and present it somewhere for easy reference.
